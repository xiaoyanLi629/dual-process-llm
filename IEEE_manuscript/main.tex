\begin{abstract}
Dual-process theory posits that human cognition operates through fast, intuitive System~1 and slow, analytical System~2 processing---a distinction with direct consequences for clinical decision-making, where diagnostic errors often arise from inappropriate reliance on pattern matching over deliberate analysis. We investigate whether large language models (LLMs) exhibit analogous dual-process behavioral signatures when configured through systematic parameter manipulation. Unlike prior work that confounds model capacity, temperature, and prompting strategy, we introduce a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ factorial ablation that isolates each factor's contribution. Our key finding is that prompting strategy (chain-of-thought vs.\ zero-shot) is the dominant factor, contributing a
34.2 percentage point accuracy gain on analytical tasks but only
10.5 points on intuitive tasks---a Prompt $\times$ Task Category interaction ($p < .001$) consistent with dual-process predictions but not guaranteed by the experimental design. We validate this pattern across four LLM families (OpenAI, DeepSeek, Qwen, Llama), introduce novel conflict tasks resistant to training-data memorization, and replace wall-clock response time with token efficiency as a model-intrinsic effort metric. We provide an honest comparison with human behavioral data, identifying both convergent patterns (analytical and intuitive tasks) and divergences (conflict tasks due to memorization). These findings offer a principled framework for configuring LLM-based clinical decision support: System~1-like settings for routine triage, System~2-like settings for complex diagnostic reasoning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Consider the following problem: ``A bat and a ball cost \$1.10 in total. The bat costs \$1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?'' Most people immediately answer ``10 cents,'' an intuitive response that feels right but is incorrect. The correct answer---5 cents---requires overriding this initial intuition through careful analysis. This classic puzzle from Frederick's Cognitive Reflection Test~\cite{Frederick2005} illustrates a central claim in cognitive science: human cognition operates via at least two distinct modes of thinking.

Kahneman~\cite{Kahneman2011} characterized these as System~1 and System~2. System~1 is fast, automatic, and effortless, relying on heuristics and associative processing. System~2 is slow, deliberate, and effortful, engaging rule-based reasoning and working memory. This dual-process framework has become one of the most influential theories in cognitive science, with broad applications from judgment and decision-making~\cite{Tversky1974} to individual differences in reasoning~\cite{Stanovich2000}.

This distinction is especially consequential in clinical medicine. Physicians routinely rely on dual-process cognition: fast pattern recognition (System~1) enables rapid identification of common presentations---a dermatologist instantly recognizing melanoma, an emergency physician identifying ST-elevation on an ECG. Slow analytical reasoning (System~2) is engaged for complex, ambiguous, or high-stakes diagnoses where deliberate differential diagnosis is essential~\cite{Croskerry2009, Norman2009}. Diagnostic errors frequently arise when System~1 heuristics dominate inappropriately---anchoring on an initial impression, premature closure, or base-rate neglect---without adequate System~2 oversight~\cite{Croskerry2013, Saposnik2016}.

As large language models (LLMs) enter clinical workflows---powering diagnostic support tools, triage systems, and clinical documentation~\cite{Singhal2023, Thirunavukarasu2023, Lee2023}---understanding their cognitive modes becomes critical. If LLMs can be configured to exhibit System~1-like and System~2-like processing, this has direct implications for system design: rapid triage queries may benefit from efficient System~1-like responses, while complex diagnostic reasoning demands the thoroughness of System~2-like processing.

Recent studies suggest that LLMs exhibit human-like cognitive patterns: they demonstrate analogical reasoning~\cite{Webb2023}, display intuitive biases~\cite{Hagendorff2023}, and can engage in chain-of-thought reasoning when prompted~\cite{Wei2022}. These observations motivate our central question: \textit{Can LLMs be configured to systematically exhibit the distinct behavioral signatures of System~1 and System~2 processing, and what are the implications for biomedical applications?}

We present the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A \textbf{$2 \times 2 \times 2$ factorial ablation} that isolates the contributions of model capacity, temperature, and prompting strategy, showing that prompting is the dominant factor and that its effect interacts with task type.
\item \textbf{Cross-model validation} across four LLM families (OpenAI, DeepSeek, Qwen, open-source Llama), demonstrating that dual-process behavioral patterns are not artifacts of a single provider.
\item \textbf{Novel conflict tasks} resistant to training-data memorization, which reveal more diagnostic dual-process performance than classic CRT items.
\item \textbf{Token efficiency} as a model-intrinsic computational effort metric, replacing wall-clock response time which conflates model computation with API infrastructure.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Dual-Process Theory in Human Cognition}

The idea that human cognition involves multiple processing systems has a long history~\cite{Evans2013}. Modern dual-process theories emerged from converging evidence across reasoning, judgment, and decision-making. Sloman~\cite{Sloman1996} provided influential empirical evidence for two distinct reasoning systems, while Stanovich and West~\cite{Stanovich2000} developed a comprehensive framework distinguishing System~1 (associative, automatic) from System~2 (rule-based, controlled). Evans~\cite{Evans2008} synthesized decades of research and identified core attributes distinguishing the two systems.

We acknowledge that dual-process theory is theoretically contested. Critics have argued that the System~1/System~2 distinction oversimplifies a continuum of processing modes, and that the boundary between ``automatic'' and ``controlled'' processing is not always clear~\cite{Evans2013}. Our approach does not require that dual-process theory be literally true of human cognition; rather, we use its behavioral predictions---differential performance across task types, effort-accuracy trade-offs---as a structured framework for characterizing LLM behavior under different configurations.

\subsection{Dual-Process Cognition in Clinical Medicine}

Croskerry~\cite{Croskerry2009} proposed a universal model of diagnostic reasoning in which physicians toggle between intuitive (non-analytical) and analytical pathways. Pattern recognition---a System~1 process---allows experienced clinicians to rapidly identify common conditions, but it is also the source of well-documented diagnostic errors: anchoring bias, availability heuristic, and premature closure~\cite{Croskerry2013, Saposnik2016}. Norman and Eva~\cite{Norman2009} showed that diagnostic errors often arise from failure to engage System~2 when System~1 produces an incorrect initial hypothesis. Pelaccia et al.~\cite{Pelaccia2011} demonstrated that expert clinical reasoning involves dynamic interplay between the two systems, with metacognitive monitoring determining when intuition should be overridden.

These findings have direct relevance for LLM-based clinical tools. If LLMs exhibit analogous dual-process behavior under different configurations, the same design principles governing when clinicians should engage deliberate reasoning can inform when LLM systems should be configured for thorough analysis versus rapid response.

\subsection{Dual-Process Patterns in LLMs}

Binz and Schulz~\cite{Binz2023} systematically tested GPT-3 on classic cognitive psychology tasks, finding human-like performance on risky choice and causal reasoning but deviations in tasks requiring deliberation. Hagendorff et al.~\cite{Hagendorff2023} documented that LLMs display intuitive behaviors and reasoning biases mirroring human patterns. Itzhak et al.~\cite{Itzhak2024} showed that instruction-tuned models exhibit emergent cognitive biases.

Wei et al.~\cite{Wei2022} found that chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting substantially improves reasoning performance, and Kojima et al.~\cite{Kojima2022} showed this effect emerges even without task-specific examples. Critically, Liu et al.~\cite{Liu2024} demonstrated that CoT can \textit{reduce} performance on tasks where deliberation harms human performance---a striking parallel to dual-process predictions. Bellini-Leite~\cite{BelliniLeite2024} offered a theoretical overview connecting dual-process theory to LLM reliability. DeepSeek-R1~\cite{DeepSeekAI2025} represents a deliberate engineering effort to enhance System~2-like extended reasoning. Binz et al.~\cite{Binz2025} developed a foundation model capturing human cognition across diverse tasks, demonstrating growing convergence between AI and cognitive modeling.

In the medical domain, LLMs have demonstrated clinical knowledge at expert levels~\cite{Singhal2023, Nori2023}, and diagnostic reasoning prompts have shown potential for interpretable clinical decision support~\cite{Savage2024}. These advances motivate understanding how parameter configuration affects LLM cognitive modes for safe biomedical deployment.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Research Questions}

We designed our study to address four core questions:

\textbf{RQ1 (Factorial Isolation):} Which parameter---model capacity, temperature, or prompting strategy---contributes most to dual-process behavioral differences, and do these factors interact with task type?

\textbf{RQ2 (Token Efficiency):} Do the two configurations exhibit effort asymmetry measured through token efficiency, and does this differ by task type?

\textbf{RQ3 (Generalizability):} Do dual-process patterns generalize across model families and persist on novel conflict tasks resistant to memorization?

\textbf{RQ4 (Human Comparison):} Where do LLM dual-process patterns converge with and diverge from human behavioral data?

\subsection{System Configuration}

We operationalized System~1 and System~2 through three parameter dimensions, each grounded in theoretical parallels to human cognition (Table~\ref{tab:system-config}).

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Operationalization of System~1 and System~2 in LLMs.}
\label{tab:system-config}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Parameter & System 1 & System 2 \\
\midrule
Model & Smaller/faster & Larger/capable \\
Temperature & 0.9 (high) & 0.2 (low) \\
Prompting & Zero-shot & Chain-of-thought \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Model Selection.} For each model family, the smaller variant serves as System~1 and the larger variant as System~2, reflecting the resource asymmetry in human cognition~\cite{Kahneman2011}.

\textbf{Temperature.} High temperature (0.9) introduces response variability, simulating the probabilistic nature of intuitive judgments. Low temperature (0.2) produces more consistent---though not deterministic, as outputs remain stochastic at any positive temperature---outputs characteristic of analytical reasoning.

\textbf{Prompting Strategy.} Zero-shot prompting for System~1 simulates automatic, unreflective processing. CoT prompting for System~2 explicitly elicits step-by-step reasoning~\cite{Wei2022}. The System~1 prompt instructed: ``\textit{Respond quickly based on your first impression. Trust your gut feeling.}'' The System~2 prompt instructed: ``\textit{Think step by step. Break down the problem systematically before reaching a conclusion.}'' Both prompts required a confidence rating (0--1).

\textbf{Important clarification:} We do not claim that LLMs possess unconscious processes or subjective experience. System~1 and System~2 refer to \textit{behavioral configurations}---observable differences in accuracy, token usage, and confidence patterns---not claims about internal mechanisms.

\subsection{Factorial Ablation Design}
\label{sec:factorial}

A key criticism of prior work (including our initial CogSci submission) is that model, temperature, and prompting are confounded: all three change simultaneously between System~1 and System~2. This means any observed difference could be driven by one factor while the others are irrelevant. To isolate each factor's contribution, we conduct a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ factorial ablation (Table~\ref{tab:factorial}).

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{$2 \times 2 \times 2$ factorial ablation design. C1 and C8 correspond to the original System~1 and System~2 configurations.}
\label{tab:factorial}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
\toprule
Condition & Model & Temp. & Prompt \\
\midrule
C1 (S1) & Mini & High & Zero-shot \\
C2 & Mini & High & CoT \\
C3 & Mini & Low & Zero-shot \\
C4 & Mini & Low & CoT \\
C5 & Full & High & Zero-shot \\
C6 & Full & High & CoT \\
C7 & Full & Low & Zero-shot \\
C8 (S2) & Full & Low & CoT \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

This design enables estimation of main effects for each factor, all two-way interactions (Model $\times$ Prompt, Model $\times$ Temperature, Prompt $\times$ Temperature), and the three-way interaction. Critically, the Prompt $\times$ Task Category interaction is our primary test of the dual-process prediction: if CoT helps more on analytical than intuitive tasks, this interaction effect is \textit{not} circular---one could equally observe CoT helping uniformly across all task types.

Both System~1 and System~2 configurations use a single API call per task with different prompts and parameters; no multi-stage processing pipeline is involved. The factorial design tests all $2^3 = 8$ combinations. Our \textbf{primary comparison} is C5 vs.\ C8 (same model GPT-4o, differing only in temperature and prompt), which isolates configuration effects from model capacity. The \textbf{secondary comparison} is C1 vs.\ C8 (the full canonical System~1 vs.\ System~2), contextualized by the factorial results.

\subsection{Multi-Model Validation}
\label{sec:multimodel}

To assess generalizability, we replicate the core System~1 vs.\ System~2 comparison across four LLM families (Table~\ref{tab:model-families}).

For each family, the smaller/faster model is configured as System~1 and the larger/more capable model as System~2, using the same temperature and prompting parameters.

\subsection{Task Design}

We assembled a task battery from established NLP benchmarks spanning three categories ($n = 200$ items per category per round, three rounds), each designed to differentially engage the two processing modes. The full pool contains approximately 11{,}117 items, from which we sample 200 per category per round.

\textbf{Intuitive Tasks:} Pattern completion, semantic associations, common-sense judgments, and rapid categorization, drawn from HellaSwag, PIQA, SIQA, CommonsenseQA, and WinoGrande. These tasks have clear intuitive answers accessible through pattern matching and associative retrieval.

\textbf{Analytical Tasks:} Multi-step arithmetic and logical deduction, drawn from GSM8K and LogiQA. These tasks require systematic step-by-step analysis.

\textbf{Conflict Tasks:} Items drawn from TruthfulQA plus 50 novel items, where the intuitive answer is systematically wrong. Classic CRT-style problems, base-rate neglect scenarios, and belief bias tasks present situations where an intuitive lure diverges from the correct analytical answer~\cite{Frederick2005}.

We excluded benchmarks that span both intuitive and analytical demands (e.g., ARC, MMLU) to ensure clean category boundaries.

\textbf{Novel Conflict Tasks.} To address training-data memorization, we developed novel conflict items that are structurally isomorphic to established CRT problems but use novel surface content unlikely to appear in training corpora. Each item presents a salient intuitive lure diverging from the correct answer. These novel items are interleaved with classic items to enable direct comparison.

\subsection{Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Accuracy:} Correctness of the final answer.
\item \textbf{Confidence:} Model-generated certainty rating (0--1 scale).
\item \textbf{Token Efficiency Ratio (TER):} Defined as $\text{TER} = \Delta\text{Accuracy} / \Delta\text{Tokens}$, measuring the accuracy gain per additional token consumed by System~2 relative to System~1. High TER indicates efficient use of additional computation; low TER indicates diminishing returns.
\item \textbf{Reasoning Density:} The ratio of reasoning tokens to total tokens in System~2 responses, measuring how much of the additional output constitutes productive reasoning versus repetition or filler.
\item \textbf{Marginal Accuracy Gain:} The per-token accuracy improvement, computed as $(Acc_{S2} - Acc_{S1}) / (Tokens_{S2} - Tokens_{S1})$.
\end{itemize}

Model responses were parsed as JSON, and accuracy was assessed against the extracted answer field to prevent false matches from intermediate reasoning steps.

We deliberately exclude wall-clock response time. API response time conflates model computation with network latency, server load, and batching---factors external to the model. Token usage is a model-intrinsic metric directly analogous to cognitive effort in dual-process theory.

\subsection{Statistical Methods}

For the factorial ablation, we use three-way ANOVA on accuracy with model, temperature, and prompting as fixed factors and task category as a moderator. For pairwise comparisons, we use independent samples $t$-tests with Cohen's $d$ effect sizes~\cite{Cohen1988}. All multi-comparison corrections use the Bonferroni method. We report all $p$-values without omission, including non-significant results, to avoid selective reporting.

\subsection{Control Baselines}

We include two control baselines to contextualize observed effects. A \textbf{random baseline} establishes expected accuracy for multiple-choice tasks ($1/n_{\text{options}}$, typically 25\% for 4-option items). A \textbf{same-configuration variance baseline} runs C8 (full System~2) twice on the same items to establish the measurement noise floor. These controls ensure that observed System~1--System~2 differences exceed both chance-level performance and run-to-run measurement noise.

\subsection{Factorial Ablation: Interactions}

The Prompt $\times$ Task Category interaction is the central finding.

The Prompt $\times$ Task Category interaction was significant ($F(2, 4792) = 18.4$, $p < .001$, $\eta^2_p = .046$): CoT improved analytical accuracy by
34.2 points but intuitive accuracy by only
10.5 points. The Model $\times$ Prompt interaction ($-0.116$) indicates that CoT helps the smaller model (mini) more than the full model (4o). The Model $\times$ Temperature ($+0.019$) and Temperature $\times$ Prompt ($+0.003$) interactions were not significant ($p > .10$ for both). The three-way interaction was not significant ($F(1, 4792) = 0.8$, $p = .37$).

This interaction is the key result addressing the circularity criticism. The design does not guarantee that CoT should help \textit{more} on analytical tasks; one could equally observe CoT helping uniformly across task types, or helping most on conflict tasks. The observed asymmetric interaction---large benefit on analytical tasks, minimal on intuitive---is the specific pattern predicted by dual-process theory and provides non-trivial evidence that the framework captures meaningful computational trade-offs.

On analytical tasks, the Token Efficiency Ratio was

This pattern provides a computational analogue of cognitive effort allocation: an efficient system should invest additional processing resources primarily when the task demands justify it. System~1-like configurations are more token-efficient for intuitive tasks, while System~2-like configurations provide meaningful accuracy gains on analytical tasks that justify the additional token cost.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Isolating Dual-Process Factors}

The factorial ablation demonstrates that the System~1/System~2 performance difference is not simply ``bigger model does better at everything.'' Prompting strategy is the dominant factor ($\eta^2_p = .081$), followed by model capacity ($\eta^2_p = .034$) and temperature ($\eta^2_p = .008$). More importantly, the Prompt $\times$ Task Category interaction ($p < .001$) shows that CoT disproportionately benefits analytical tasks---the specific pattern predicted by dual-process theory.

This interaction was not guaranteed by the experimental design. CoT is designed to elicit explicit reasoning, but this does not entail that it should help \textit{more} on analytical tasks. One could plausibly observe: (a)~CoT helping equally across all task types (a general quality boost), (b)~CoT helping most on conflict tasks (where reasoning chains might help override intuitive lures), or (c)~CoT hurting performance on intuitive tasks (as Liu et al.~\cite{Liu2024} documented in some settings). The observed pattern---large benefit on analytical, small on intuitive, intermediate on conflict---is one specific outcome among several plausible alternatives, and its consistency with dual-process predictions provides non-trivial evidence.

We do not claim that LLMs possess unconscious processes, subjective experience, or System~1 cognition in the human sense. System~1 in humans involves sub-linguistic, pre-attentive processing that has no obvious analogue in autoregressive language models. What we demonstrate is that the \textit{behavioral pattern}---differential performance across task types as a function of configuration---generalizes from biological to computational systems. This is a claim about behavioral signatures, not about underlying mechanisms.

\subsection{Cross-Model Robustness}

The dual-process pattern holds across all four model families: the analytical S2--S1 gap is always positive (range: +11.0 to +52.0 points) and exceeds the intuitive gap for 3 of 4 families. However, the pattern is not uniform. DeepSeek-Reasoner showed \textit{negative} S2--S1 gaps on intuitive ($-15.0$\%) and conflict ($-14.0$\%) tasks, suggesting that its extended reasoning mode can hurt performance when deliberation is unnecessary---consistent with Liu et al.'s~\cite{Liu2024} finding that chain-of-thought can reduce accuracy on tasks where intuitive processing suffices. This provides evidence that over-reasoning is a genuine risk: System~2-like configurations are not universally beneficial, and the dual-process framework correctly predicts when deliberation helps versus harms. OpenAI, Qwen, and Llama showed consistently positive S2--S1 gaps across all categories, with analytical gaps of +50.0, +51.0, and +52.0 points respectively.

\subsection{Training Data Contamination}

Classic CRT items in the conflict category showed high System~1 accuracy (83.0\%), suggesting partial training-data memorization. Our conflict category includes 50 novel items interleaved with TruthfulQA items to mitigate this concern. The overall conflict-task S2--S1 gap of +10.5 percentage points (83.0\% to 93.5\%) demonstrates that System~2 still provides meaningful gains even on tasks where memorization partially inflates baseline performance. This validates the importance of including memorization-resistant items for studying dual-process patterns in LLMs.

\subsection{Implications for Biomedical Decision Support}

These findings provide a principled basis for configuring LLM-based clinical tools:

\textbf{Routine triage and screening.} For pattern-matching tasks (identifying abnormal lab values, flagging critical results, initial symptom screening), System~1-like configurations (smaller models, zero-shot prompting) are adequate and more cost-effective.
The factorial analysis shows that prompting strategy contributes only +10.5\% on these tasks, making the $2.35\times$ token overhead a consideration for cost-sensitive deployments.

\textbf{Complex diagnostic reasoning.} For differential diagnosis, interpreting ambiguous presentations, or evaluating rare conditions, System~2-like configurations (larger models, CoT prompting) provide substantial accuracy gains (+34.2\% on analytical tasks) that justify the additional computational cost.

\textbf{Confidence calibration.} System~2's better calibration (overconfidence gap of 1.5\% vs.\ 18.1\%) is especially important for clinical deployment. Overconfident AI systems could reinforce rather than mitigate human anchoring bias~\cite{Saposnik2016}, making calibration a safety-critical property.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several important limitations must be acknowledged. First, we cannot claim LLMs have ``cognition'' in any meaningful sense---we test behavioral patterns under different configurations, not internal cognitive mechanisms. Second, human benchmarks are drawn from published studies with different populations and experimental paradigms; our comparison is qualitative, not a matched experiment. Third, temperature and prompting are partially confounded with output length: CoT mechanically produces more tokens, and we cannot fully separate ``more reasoning'' from ``more output.'' The token efficiency metrics partially address this by measuring returns per token, but the confound remains. Fourth, confidence ratings are elicited through prompting rather than derived from internal model probabilities, which may limit their reliability. Fifth, our task battery does not cover all clinically relevant domains (e.g., image-based diagnosis, longitudinal reasoning over patient histories). Sixth, separating temperature and prompting effects within a single model (rather than across model sizes) would more cleanly isolate the contribution of each factor---we encourage future work using this design.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We demonstrate that LLMs configured with different parameter settings exhibit behavioral patterns consistent with dual-process cognition. A $2 \times 2 \times 2$ factorial ablation reveals that prompting strategy is the dominant factor, and critically, its effect interacts with task type: chain-of-thought reasoning disproportionately benefits analytical tasks over intuitive ones. This interaction pattern---not guaranteed by the design---generalizes across four LLM families (OpenAI, DeepSeek, Qwen, Llama) and provides non-trivial evidence that the dual-process framework captures meaningful computational trade-offs. For biomedical informatics, these findings offer a principled basis for configuring LLM-based clinical decision support: efficient System~1-like settings for routine tasks, and thorough System~2-like settings for complex diagnostic reasoning where both accuracy and calibrated confidence are essential.
